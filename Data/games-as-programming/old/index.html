Games as ProgrammingWhen games stopped being products.

Netflix closed five game studios in five years and kept every category that behaves like an episode. Then it premiered the GTA VI reveal through its documentaries department, and a trailer for an unreleased game became the most-watched thing on the service that week in 87 of 93 countries.

This report tests one hypothesis across three companies: games are being reclassified from products you seek out into programming that arrives, and the filter being applied is not genre or quality but whether a thing behaves like an episode. Amazon and Microsoft reached the same conclusion on the same afternoon in July, independently. Six weeks later Microsoft put a meter on it.

Part I tests it against what Netflix shut and kept. Part II tests it against Amazon, Microsoft, and market data that disagrees with itself by nearly six times. Part III tests it against the compute the whole model runs on — and against this report's own failed drafts.

None of these four numbers is what it looks like. A Netflix view is total hours watched divided by runtime, not a headcount, over a four-day window. The 11x is growth off a base Netflix has never published, and Peters declined to give gameplay hours when asked on the same call. The 4% is Microsoft's own unaudited characterisation of its own subscriber base, released alongside a price change. The five closures span five years and four different rationales. None of these figures are additively comparable, and none is comparable to a unit-sales number.

The filter was never about genre or quality. Every studio Netflix exited built games as products — things a person has to go and get, install, learn, and return to. Every category it kept makes something that arrives inside an app the household was already in. Team Blue was building a big-budget console action game; Alain Tascan said publicly that the project belonged to the console or PC world and was not adapted to the platform Netflix works in. The industry heard a retreat from games because that was the sentence it was expecting.
Three claims in this report were removed in review, and the removals are in the text rather than in a footnote. A four-step chain in which players priced out of a $799.99 console substitute streaming died on Microsoft's own disclosure that the cap affects 4% of subscribers. A rebuttal to Joost van Dreunen resting on compute scarcity died because compute is rentable and intellectual property is not, which makes scarcity an argument for his position. And calling the 19 November GTA 6 launch "the test" was wrong: a console-exclusive megahit is compatible with this thesis and with its exact opposite, so it discriminates between nothing. Part III carries all three corrections and replaces the last one with five dated conditions.

The instrument that would settle most of this argument is the one nobody publishes: cloud session length, and Netflix game hours per subscriber. Peters was asked for the second in July and declined. Until those numbers exist, every thesis in this space is inference dressed as measurement, including this one.
