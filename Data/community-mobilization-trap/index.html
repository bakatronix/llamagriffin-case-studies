The Mobilization TrapCommunity acquires. It does not hold.

Four live-service games acquired forty-eight million players between them and shut down. Two governments fell inside a week and lost their countries within six months. This suite argues that the same structural gap does both: mass coordination became cheap and fast, and the multi-year organizational labour that converts coordination into durability did not get cheaper at all.

Participation infrastructure is neither necessary nor sufficient for retention. Eleven of the twelve failed cases in this report shipped a genuine player-authoring layer, and Worlds Adrift shipped its island editor more than a year before the game itself, peaking at 2,057 concurrent. Meanwhile three of the longest-lived games in the sample, Valheim, Path of Exile and Helldivers 2, never shipped one at all. What separates them is sustained content cadence over years, which is unglamorous, expensive, and almost never funded at greenlight.
This report was commissioned to test a thesis and a team was tasked with destroying it. Six of eight component claims fell. The replacement claim then failed its own second review for selecting on the outcome, and a third pass falsified the replacement's central mechanism. All three corrections are in the text rather than in a footnote, because the corrections are more useful than the original argument was.

The instrument that would settle most of this argument is the one nobody publishes: what a launch cost to market, next to what it earned. Until that number exists, every thesis in this space is inference dressed as measurement, including this one.
