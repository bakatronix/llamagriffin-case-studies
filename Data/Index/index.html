The Price of Play Money.

In 2025, people put $6.8 billion into Robux alone and creators earned more than $1.5 billion from it, both the company's own numbers. The generation holding the rest is a few years from its first salaries.

Last Christmas the most requested gift among American children was in-game currency: 43% of kids aged five to seventeen, according to the ESA. This is the first money a generation actually holds, and nobody was keeping a public record of what it costs them.

This desk tracks what that money is actually worth: one benchmark bundle per currency, priced in every market that sells it, converted daily, measured against the US. Built for the parents paying in it, the studios pricing against it, and the analysts covering the first economy a generation grew up inside.

This is Big Mac Index but for game currencies, rebuilt for the first money a generation actually holds.

What This Is

The Big Mac Index of Game Currencies

Because parents are giving allowances in Robux.

The Economist has tracked the price of a Big Mac across countries since 1986. The logic is simple: one product, sold everywhere, reveals how far purchasing power stretches across markets. This index applies the same move to game currencies.

In households across twelve countries, parents top up Robux, V-Bucks, and other premium currencies as a regular allowance. The amounts feel similar. The real cost is not. A parent in India topping up 800 Robux spends the equivalent of $8.29. A parent in the United Kingdom spends $12.15 for the same thing. Neither parent is looking at exchange rates. They are looking at a number their child asked for.

This index tracks that gap daily, across every major market where these currencies are sold. It uses the same FX source, the same confidence-tagging system, and the same normalization formula for every currency it covers. The question it answers is not which store is cheapest. It is who is being asked to pay the most real money for the same unit of digital play, and how far that gap moves when nobody is watching.

The Method

Common Framework

One formula across all indexes.

Every page in the series uses the same calculation, the same FX source, and the same confidence-tagging system. This is what allows the homepage to compare across entirely different game currencies without bundle-size distortion.

The normalization step

Robux, V-Bucks, Minecoins, and Oneiric Shards have different bundle sizes and very different per-unit economics. Different pack sizes lie when compared raw. Everything on this desk is normalized to the cost of 100 base units before any comparison happens. The series therefore normalizes every benchmark to cost per 100 base units before comparing across currencies.

Convert to USD
Implied USD = Local Price ÷ FX Rate
Normalize per unit
Implied USD / unit = Implied USD ÷ Bundle Size
Scale to 100 units
Implied USD / 100u = Implied USD / unit × 100
Measure vs US baseline
Premium / Discount % = ( Local ÷ US − 1 ) × 100
Base units only. The face value on the pack. Bonuses and promotional extras are secondary panels, never rows. Standard always-on bonus units, first-time double bonuses, and promotional extras are excluded from the primary calculation. They appear as secondary panels on child pages, not in the main ranking.

Data rules

Every market uses the identical bundle size. Every market prices the identical pack. Tier substitution would smuggle the distortion straight back in.

A promotion is not a price. Launch bonuses and monthly passes are secondary panels on the child pages.

Mid-market rates blended from 84 central banks, refreshed daily, no key, no vendor. Every page draws from the same well.

The index compares the price displayed on each regional storefront. UK and EU tags include VAT. That premium is disclosed on each page, not normalized away, because tax is part of what a player pays.

Official, Confirmed, Third-party provisional, Contested. Lower confidence, higher bar before a move prints.

The dirham and its siblings appear in the tables but not the drift charts. A peg has no story to tell.

Why It Matters

The Reframe

Store credit, dressed as money.

Every currency on this desk borrows the grammar of money: the coin, the wallet, the balance in the corner of the screen. Almost none of them grants the one right that makes money money, which is the right to leave. A generation is learning what a price is from inside these systems. Somebody should be keeping the ledger.
