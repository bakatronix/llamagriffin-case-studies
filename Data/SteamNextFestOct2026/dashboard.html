Read the flux like a trader, not a fan: by the final weekend the field has split into three groups. If a title's peak is already hours or days behind it by the weekend, its discovery window has effectively closed — the wishlists it's adding now are the long tail.

The percentage trap: The bottom group showed the highest percentage growth of any tier (12.5%) — but from a median of just 15 followers, that's one extra person. A spike in the final week looks good on a chart and does almost nothing for your peak. Track raw gains, not percentages.

What this means in practice

Do

  • Build the base for months. Wishlist campaigns, festival slots and press in the months before a February demo beat a burst in late January.
  • Count absolute followers. If you gained 40 last week, write down 40 — not "50%."
  • Check your genre's conversion table and budget pre-fest marketing accordingly.
  • Disclose AI honestly. Top-ranked games that disclosed still performed.

Avoid

  • Relying on the fest to create discovery. With 7,240 games it amplifies, it doesn't launch.
  • Leaving a demo up for weeks without followers. If it hasn't built an audience by fest time, it likely won't during it.
  • Reading percentage growth as momentum. From a tiny base it's noise.
  • Assuming scale here equals sales later. These are peak demo players, not wishlists.

Section 09

What to Do Before February

The data points one direction: start earlier.

The next edition is expected in February. If you're planning to enter, October's data suggests a concrete sequence of actions — none of them last-minute.

Start now, not in January

  • Count followers every week. Base and momentum both predict early peak about equally; both take time to build.
  • Schedule wishlist campaigns, festival slots and press for Nov–Dec. That does more for a February demo than a burst of posts in late January.

Match your genre's math

  • If few demos in your genre reach 10 players (platformers, walking sims), budget more pre-festival marketing — or delay to a later fest to build a bigger base.
  • If you're in co-op, party, survival-craft or desktop/idle, lead with that hook in the first 10 seconds of the trailer.

Measure the right thing

  • Track raw follower gains, not percentages. A 50% jump from 80 to 120 won't move your peak.
  • Have your demo live early — the top 25 ran demos ~19 days before the fest; don't leave one up for weeks silently.

Use the tooling

  • Zukowski's free Next Fest tracker lets you enter your traffic and wishlist numbers and compare them with the Feb and Jun 2026 editions — the quickest reality check against your follower count.
  • Watch for the post-fest follow-up on total follower gains.

Read these caveats before citing numbers

Peak demo players ≠ wishlists. Steam does not publish per-game wishlist data. Follower counts are a public stand-in; wishlist tallies on third-party trackers are estimates.

Early-festival data. Zukowski's correlation analysis measures demo peak CCU for 12–21 October only; his follower-gain follow-up lands after the festival closes. Treat the "what predicted early peak" findings as provisional.

Self-reported AI disclosure. The ~35% figure counts only developers who chose to disclose; the true rate could be higher or lower.

Lineup-number variance. 7,240 (HTMAG scrape), 7,317 (Output Lag), and ~8,000 (press preview). Differences come from timing, withdrawals, and 18+ hidden titles.

Projections are directional. The ~51.7% ratio (15,158 entries ÷ ~29,300 projected releases) and any 2026 release-count figures rest on estimates; a game entering multiple editions counts once per edition.

Correlation, not causation. Followers and momentum correlate with early peak; neither is proven to cause it. Game quality, marketing and genre all interact.

Seven thousand games, one pattern. The festival got bigger; the way to get seen did not — bring an audience, or bring real momentum, before it starts. Just don't show up empty.
