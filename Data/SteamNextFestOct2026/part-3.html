The Follower Decider

You are who you were before the fest.

Zukowski grouped every October demo by peak concurrent players, then looked at their Steam follower count the day before the festival. Steam followers are a public stand-in for wishlists — which only the developer can see — and the gradient is stark: the winning demos arrived with an audience already banked.

Section 02

What Actually Predicts Peak Players

Momentum helps; a base is everything.

Zukowski tested which number best tracks a demo's eventual peak players. Total followers won; final-week catch came close; final-week percentage growth was slightly negative.

The percentage trap: the bottom group showed the highest percentage growth of any tier (~12.5%) — but from a median of 15 followers, that's one extra person. A final-week spike looks good on a chart and does almost nothing for your peak. Track raw gains, not percentages.

Read through the lens of Parts I and II, the implication is coherent: scale makes the base the whole game. The festival's engagement-ranked Day 3+ phase rewards the demos already converting — which are the ones that entered with followers. Momentum is a multiplier applied to a funnel that was either already full or already empty.

Section 03

AI Disclosure — Concentrated at the Bottom

Disclosure doesn't doom a game; it clusters where attention is thinnest.

Steam requires developers to declare generative AI use on the store page. Zukowski counted 2,502 games (~35%) with a disclosure in October 2026 — self-reported, so the true rate could differ — but the split by performance tier is unambiguous.

Correlation is not causation. There's no evidence that disclosing AI causes weak results — plenty of low-ranked games disclosed nothing, and AI-disclosed titles did reach the top 25 (Abyssal Lapidary, on the most-played list, discloses AI). What the data shows is that most disclosed AI work sits in the crowded bottom of the festival. If your game uses AI art, it's competing inside that crowd — which is precisely why a pre-built audience matters more, not less.
Section 04

The February Playbook

Start earlier, count the right number.

The next edition is expected in February. October's data points to a concrete sequence of actions — none of them last-minute — distilled into what to start and what to stop.

Start now, not in January

  • Count followers every week. The correlation favours a base built over months.
  • Schedule wishlist campaigns, festival slots and press for Nov–Dec. That does more for a February demo than a late-January burst of posts.
  • Ship the demo early. The top 25 ran demos ~19 days before the fest — but only when an audience was already converting.

Match your genre's math

  • If few demos in your genre reach 10 players (platformers, walking sims), budget more pre-festival marketing — or delay to a later fest to build a bigger base.
  • If you're in co-op, party, survival-craft or desktop/idle, lead with that hook in the first ten seconds of the trailer.

Measure the right thing

  • Track raw follower gains, not percentages. A 50% jump from 80 to 120 won't move your peak.
  • Set a "day-before-fest" baseline and judge the festival against it, not against the leaderboard.

Use the tooling

  • Zukowski's free Next Fest tracker compares your traffic and wishlist numbers against the Feb and Jun 2026 editions.
  • Be honest on AI disclosure. Steam requires it, and top-ranked games that disclosed still performed.
  • Watch for the post-fest follow-up on total follower gains once this edition closes.
The through-line of the whole case study: three years of growth, an 8× field, and a two-tier result — all resolved by a single, boring truth. The festival stopped deciding who wins sometime around 2024. Now it only reveals who already had. Arrive with an audience.
