# Llama & Griffin — Case Study Executive Summary

Seven intelligence reports on the games industry. The through-line: the market keeps measuring the wrong number, and the structural shifts studios keep mispricing are the ones that actually decide outcomes — from storefront economics to AI, from discovery to monetization, and from audience acquisition to the part that makes any of it last.

---

## 1. Mobile vs Premium: The Store-by-Store Intelligence Report

**What it argues.** "Steam revenue" describes at least five different measurements depending on the firm and scope cited. Two structurally different economies — mobile free-to-play and premium PC/console — share a confused vocabulary, and the divergence traces to one fork: does the sale happen at download, or weeks after it. The real question is which economy matches your game, not which store is cheaper.

**Structure.** Part I (The Framework: FTUE, commissions, UA economics, launch decision model) · Part II (Premium storefronts: the Steam revenue discrepancy, Valve's tiered royalty, the real Epic gap) · Part III (Mobile storefronts & synthesis: App Store/Google Play, the decision matrix).

**Key findings.** On 2024 alone, Alinea says $15.4B and GameDiscoverCo says $10.8B — a **$4.6B gap on a completed year**; 37:1 Steam-to-Epic third-party revenue; 79% of Steam revenue now from back-catalogue. Commissions: Steam 30% (25% >$10M, 20% >$50M, blended ~24%), Epic 12% (0% on first $1M/yr since June 2025), Apple/Google 30% with a 15% small-developer tier. The core fork: premium FTUE justifies a purchase (the 2-hour/14-day refund clock), mobile FTUE earns a second session — and their failure modes are opposite timing errors.

**So what.** Platform revenue toplines are "actively misleading" as a greenlight input. Effective rate (not headline rate) and who owns the demand channel decide the real cut; pricing a Steam launch with Epic's math, or a mobile funnel with Steam's refund logic, applies the wrong economic model.

## 2. The Founder's Playbook: Building a Game Studio That Survives What's Already Happening

**What it argues.** Record revenue, collapsed funding, and saturated supply are simultaneously true — not a contradiction in a cycle but features of a structural bifurcation. Studios greenlit in 2022–24 are walking into a market that no longer resembles their original brief.

**Structure.** Part I (The Fracture: revenue paradox, supply problem, funding reset) · Part II (The Funnel: degraded conversion, Early Access no longer a funding bridge) · Part III (The Playbook: five structural rules, a funding map, a six-question diagnostic).

**Key findings.** **$195.6B** 2025 global content revenue, +5.3% YoY (3rd record). But **21,541** Steam releases in 2025 (highest ever) against a **median indie gross of $249** ($174 after Valve's cut); 66%+ earned under $1,000. Funding: pre-seed fell from ~$400M/qtr (2021) to under $100M/qtr (2025). Wishlist-to-purchase is 5–10% in 2026; 52% of GDC respondents now call GenAI's impact negative (up from 18% in 2024).

**So what.** Aggregate revenue describes an industry, not studio-level odds. A studio financed against the median outcome is financed for a market that doesn't exist — build for a low-capital, mid-market-squeezed reality.

## 3. The Three Machines: AI as Gaming's Infrastructure, Rulebook, and Invoice

**What it argues.** The US, China, and the EU are not building one AI industry at three speeds but three machines for three purposes. Game development is downstream of all three, so AI arrives simultaneously as production infrastructure, regulation, and cost structure — and most studios have priced only the first. The model you pick in pre-production is now a market-access decision.

**Structure.** Part I (The Pipeline: what AI actually sits at today, tier framework) · Part II (The Split: three regulatory machines) · Part III (The Compression: AI data centres, console price cascades, three squeezes landing as one event).

**Key findings.** GDC 2026: **36% use GenAI at work but 52% call its impact negative** (7:1 negative-to-positive). Only **5% of AI usage is player-facing** (vs 81% research/ideation). Adoption is workflow-layer and "load-bearing," but the tools are a **dependency, not a tool** — on providers and on diverging regulation (0 Western models on China's whitelist). AI Next Fest disclosure rose 21.2% → 26.5%.

**So what.** AI's first measurable effect isn't better games but **more games competing for a fixed pool of attention** — raising the value of the one asset tools can't produce: a hand-built audience. Pipeline tool choice is now a legal/market-access decision.

## 4. Steam Next Fest: Three Years, One Pattern

**What it argues.** The festival grew from ~900 to ~4,900 active demos and in doing so stopped creating breakouts — it now amplifies the ones that arrive with an audience already built, turning the event into a two-tier competition.

**Structure.** Part I (Three-Year Arc: seven editions, the co-op pattern, the discovery-compression crisis) · Part II (June 2026 in Depth) · Part III (The AI Disclosure Crisis).

**Key findings.** **~4.4x demo growth 2023→2026** and roughly a **20x fall in impressions per game** (the same game on 900 games got 900,000 impressions/1,600 WL; on ~4,900 games, 58,000/350 — a 15x reduction). June 2026: 4,931 demos, **true median ~200 wishlists**. **10k+ pre-fest wishlists to break out**; **co-op is the single most reliable multiplier** (raises median 3x+). AI disclosure hit 26.5% yet AI-disclosed games stayed out of the top tier.

**So what.** The fest amplifies momentum; it doesn't supply it. Viable entry now needs pre-fest momentum (2k+ WL minimum, 8–12k target), an early demo, a 20–60 min arc, co-op where design allows, and Simplified Chinese localisation — otherwise the game is algorithm-invisible after Day 2.

## 5. Gen Alpha: The Audience You're Pricing Already Left

**What it argues.** Gen Alpha is the first generation raised entirely inside the casino economy — fluent in monetization mechanics before it was fluent in money. The market is already moving toward participation, but most studios are still pricing the gameplay loop while giving away the participation layer that actually compounds.

**Structure.** Part I (The Mechanism: casino-economy literacy) · Part II (The Behavior: the observable fan output) · Part III (The Consequence: price-tier collapse, the underbuilt participation product).

**Key findings.** Roblox **380M MAU**, **144M DAU** (+69% YoY), **10B+ hours/month** — exceeding Steam+PlayStation+Fortnite combined; **35% of verified DAU under 13**. The mechanism is timing-based, not character-based: a system encountered at formation age produces a different relationship than the same system met as an adult. They audit a new game's shop before deciding whether they like it. Contested conclusion: the **Resistance reading** (literacy breeds caution) vs the **Efficiency reading** (extraction survives but must be honest). First Gen Alpha cohort turns 13 in 2027.

**So what.** Player models were written for a customer who met monetization as an adult with a formed money-sense — that customer is aging out. The participation layer (audience building, governing, treating the game as a place rather than a purchase) is the product almost no one is building, and it's what retains the graduating Roblox cohort.

## 6. China, In Three Parts

**What it argues.** The Western question "can we sell there?" is obsolete. China became the largest games market in 2025 and then produced the audience and formats that set the terms everyone else plays by — the market didn't ignore you; you ignored it.

**Structure.** Part I (The Market: China as largest market, the China multiplier) · Part II (The Audience: the gacha paradox) · Part III (The Product: microdrama-with-a-controller, the breakout FMV genre).

**Key findings.** **$50.1B** China domestic market 2025 (+7.68%); **683M** registered gamers. **50%+ of Steam users were Chinese-speaking in Feb 2025**; Simplified Chinese is consistently the #2 Steam language. Localized titles see **20–60%+ of Steam sales from China** — Stellar Blade ~58% of 1M PC copies in 72h; for a $50M title, localisation could add $20–30M for a $100k–500k spend. Anti-case Forza Motorsport: zero Chinese content, reflexive ¥328 markdown, peaked at 4,703 CCU, franchise cancelled in July 2025. Black Myth: Wukong: 25M+ copies, $1B+ Steam revenue in 2 months, ~70% of sales from China.

**So what.** For premium PC studios China is the single highest-leverage market access in the industry: regional pricing per Steam's framework, Simplified Chinese localisation, culturally relevant content, presence on Bilibili/Weibo/Douyin, and entering Next Fest China-ready. Skipping it is a self-imposed handicap that has already killed a franchise.

## 7. The Community Mobilization Trap

**What it argues.** Four live-service games acquired 48 million players and shut down anyway; two governments fell within a week of mass protest and lost control of the country within six months. Different domains, same failure: both mobilized people fast and cheap using tools built for exactly that, and neither converted that mobilization into something that lasted. Coordination has gotten radically cheaper; the organizational work that turns a crowd into a durable institution — a retained playerbase, a functioning government — still takes years, still costs money, and cannot be skipped.

**Structure.** Part I (The Acquisition Trap: four shutdowns and the participation-infrastructure hypothesis) · Part II (The Signal Is Not the Cause: the community-signal literature) · Part III (Succession: Nepal and Madagascar, plus the year-three diagnostic).

**Key findings.** **48M players** acquired across four titles that shut down; **0.15x** median Steam wishlist-to-week-one conversion; **11 of 12** failed cases shipped a genuine player-authoring layer while three of the longest-lived games (Valheim, Path of Exile, Helldivers 2) never shipped one. Peak shape describes a regime, not a forecast. Nepal and Madagascar both won in under a week and lost the country that followed within eighteen months. Reading tied to the instrument nobody publishes: **what a launch cost to market, next to what it earned.**

**So what.** Participation infrastructure is neither necessary nor sufficient for retention — what separates the survivors is sustained content cadence over years, which is unglamorous, expensive, and almost never funded at greenlight. Coordination got cheap; durability did not. Until the launch-cost-to-revenue number exists, every thesis in this space is inference dressed as measurement.

---

## The common thread

Across all seven reports, the pattern is the same: the industry optimizes for the cheap, measurable, front-loaded variable — acquisition, discovery, headline revenue, the buyable part — while the durable, decisive variable (retention, cadence, participation, hand-built audience, market access) is expensive, slower, and almost never funded at the moment it matters. The reports keep coming back to the same correction: measure the right number, price the right model, and assume the cheap half is exactly where the margin isn't.
